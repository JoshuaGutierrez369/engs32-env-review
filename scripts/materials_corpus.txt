Pollution   
•  Pollution  is  the  undesirable  change   in  the  physical,  chemical,  or    biological  characteristics  of  an   ecosystem  resulting  in  diminished   quality  and  utility  of  a  resource.   •  Anything  that  makes  the    environment  undesirable  and   unclean  is  referred  to  as  pollutants .  
Pollution   

Major  types  of  pollution:  •  water  pollution   •  air  pollution   •  litter   •  light  pollution   •  noise  pollution   •  plastic  pollution   •  soil  contamination   •  radioactive  contamination  •  thermal  pollution   •  visual  pollution  
Water  Resources  and   

Water  Pollution  Water  Consumption   
According  to  Article  10,  Water  Code  of  the  Philippines,  water  may   be  appropriated  for  the  following  purposes:   •  Domestic   •  Municipal   •  Irrigation   •  Power  generation   •  Fisheries   •  Livestock  raising   •  Industrial   •  Recreational,  and   •  Other  purposes  
Domestic   
•  Use  of  water  for  domestic  purposes  -  the  utilization  of  water  for   drinking,  washing,  bathing,  cooking  or  other  household  needs,   home  gardens,  and  watering  of  lawns  or  domestic  animals.  
 

Municipal   
•  Use  of  water  for  municipal  purposes  -  the  utilization  of  water  for   supplying  the  water  requirements  of  the  community.   
 

Industrial   
•  Use  of  water  for  industrial  purposes  -  the  utilization  of  water  in   factories,  industrial  plants  and  mines,  including  the  use  of  water   as  an  ingredient  of  a  finished  product.  
 

Power  Generation   
•  Use  of  water  for  power  generation  -  the  utilization  of  water  for   producing  electrical  or  mechanical  power.   
 

Irrigation   
•  Use  of  water  for  irrigation  -  the  utilization  of  water  for  producing   agricultural  crops.  
 

Fisheries   
•  Use  of  water  for  fisheries  -  the  utilization  of  water  for  the   propagation  and  culture  of  fish  as  a  commercial  enterprise.  
 

Livestock  Raising   
•  Use  of  water  for  livestock  raising  -  the  utilization  of  water  for   large  herds  or  flocks  of  animals  raised  as  a  commercial   enterprise.   
 

Recreational   
•  Use  of  water  for  recreational  purposes  -  the  utilization  of  water   for  swimming  pools,  bath  houses,  boating,  water  skiing,  golf   courses  and  other  similar  facilities  in  resorts  and  other  places  of   recreation.   

 
Water  Consumption   
 Worldwide,  agriculture  accounts  for  70%  of  all  water  consumption,  compared  to  20%  for   industry  and  10%  for  domestic  use

Water  Quality   
•  Physical  Characteristics   •  Turbidity,  Temperature,  Color,  taste,  and  odor  •  Chemical  Characteristics   •  Biological  Characteristics  
Turbidity   
•  When  small  particles  are  suspended   in  water,  they  tend  to  scatter  and   absorb  light  rays.  This  gives  the   water  a  murky  or  turbid  
appearance,   and  this  effect  is  called  turbidity.   •  Turbidity  is  a  measure  of  the  light  
transmitting
 
properties
 
of
 
water
 
and
 
is
 
  comprised  of  suspended  and    colloidal  material.  It  is  important   parameter  of  drinking  water  quality   for  health  and  aesthetic  reasons.  
Turbidity   
•  Turbidity  is  measured  in  units  that  relate   the  clarity  of  water  sample  to  that  of  a   standardized  suspension  of  
silica.    •  The  interference  in  the  passage  of  light   caused  by  a  suspension  of  1  mg/L  of   silica  is  equivalent  to  one  turbidity  unit   or  NTU  (Nephelometric  Turbidity   Units)   •  The  instrument  used  for  measuring  it  is   called  Nephelometer  or  Turbidimeter.  
Turbidity   

Color   
•  Color  may  be  caused  by  dissolved  or  suspended  colloidal   particles  primarily  from  decaying  leaves  or  microscopic  plants.   •  Colored  water  give  the  appearance  of  being  unfit  to  drink,  even   though  the  water  may  be  perfectly  safe  for  public  use.   
•  True  Color  &  Apparent  Color  
Temperature   
Water  temperature  is  one  of  the  most   important  characteristics  of  an  aquatic   system,  affecting:   •  Dissolved  oxygen  levels .  The   solubility  of  oxygen  decreases  as   water  temperature  increases.   •  Chemical  processes .  Temperature   affects  the  solubility  and  reaction   rates  of  chemicals.  In  general,  the   rate  of  chemical  reactions  increases   with  increasing  water  temperature.  
Temperature   
Water  temperature  is  one  of  the  most   important  characteristics  of  an  aquatic   system,  affecting:   •  Biological  processes .  Temperature   affects  metabolism,  growth,  and   reproduction.   •  Species  composition  of  the   aquatic  ecosystem .  Many  aquatic   species  can  survive  only  within  a   limited  temperature  range.  
Odor  and  Taste   
•  Hydrogen  sulfide,  H
2
S,  is  a  common   cause  of  odor  in  water  supplies.  The   rotten-egg  smell  of  this  gas  may  be   encountered  in  water  that  has  been  in   contact  with  naturally  occurring  deposit   of  decaying  organic  matter.   •  Water  tastes  bitter  when  contaminated   with  alkaline  impurities  and  salty  when   the  impurities  are  metallic  salts.   •  Taste  and  odor  are  human  perceptions  of   water  quality.  
Water  Quality   
•  Physical  Characteristics   •  Chemical  Characteristics   •  Dissolved  Oxygen,  Biochemical  Oxygen  Demand,  Solids,  Acidity,   Alkalinity,  and  pH   •  Biological  Characteristics  
Dissolved  Oxygen    
•  Dissolved  oxygen  refers  to  the   amount  of  oxygen  present  in    water  needed  by  aquatic    organism  to  survive.  The  higher   the  concentration  of  dissolved    
oxygen  is,  the  better  the  water    quality.   •  DO  has  no  direct  effect  on    public  health.  But,  drinking    water  with  very  little  or  no    oxygen  tastes  flat.  
Biochemical  Oxygen  Demand   
•  Bacterial  and  other  microorganisms  use  organic   substance  for  food.  As  they  metabolize  organic   materials,  they  consume  oxygen.  The  organic    are  broken  down  into  simple  compounds.   •  The  BOD  is  used  as  an  indirect  measure  of  the   
total  amount  of  biodegradable  organics  in  water.    •  The  more  organic  material  there  is  in  water,  the   higher  the  BOD  exerted  by  the  microbes  will  be.  
Solids   
•  Solids  occur  in  water  either  in   solution  or  in  suspension.  These   two  types  of  solids  are    distinguished  by  passing  the   water  through  a  glass-fiber  filter.  •  Suspended  solids  are  retained  on   top  of  the  filter    •  Dissolved  solids  pass  through  the   filter  with  the  water.  
pH   
•  pH  is  a  measure  of  the  intensity  of  the  acidity  or   the  alkalinity.  The  primary  reason  for  measuring   the  pH  of  water  is  to  able  to  control  the  water   treatment  process  in  the  water  purification   facility.    •  Alkalinity  is  defined  as  the  capacity  of  natural   water  to  neutralize  acid  added  to  it.  Total   alkalinity  is  the  amount  of  acid  required  to  reach   a  specific  pH.   •  Acidity  is  the  "quantitative  capacity  of  aqueous   media  to  react  with  hydroxyl  ions".  Acidity   indicates  the  corrosiveness  
of  acidic  water  on   steel,  concrete  and  other  materials.

Sources  of  Water  Pollutants   
•  Point  sources  -  harmful  substances  are  emitted  directly  into  a   body  of  water    •  Inadequately  treated  domestic  wastewater  or  sewage,  agricultural   wastewater,  industrial  wastewater   •  Example:  A  pipe  dumping  factory  waste  into  a  nearby  river  •  Non-point  sources  -  relatively  scattered  or  diffused,  having  no   specific  location   •  Rain-  and  groundwater  runoff  from  solid  waste  or  garbage  deposits,   which  is  also  known  as  leachate.   
•  Example:  Agricultural  runoff  which  carries  fertilizers  and  pesticides  
Types  of  Water  Pollutants   
•  Sediments  -  tiny  bits  of  soil,  rocks  or  other  solid  particles  that   are  washed  or  blown  from  land  into  the  water.   •  Infectious  agents  –  water  polluted  by  pathogenic  bacteria,   viruses  and  protozoan    •  Diseases  transmitted  via  water  include  viral  hepatitis,  polio  (viral),   typhoid  fever  (bacterial),  amoebic  dysentery  (protozoan),  cholera   (bacterial),  schistosomiasis  (parasitic  worm)  and  salmonellosis   (bacterial)  
Types  of  Water  Pollutants   
•  Organic  chemicals  -  include  solvents,    pesticides  and  organic  fertilizers   •  Inorganic  chemicals  –  include  metals,    acids  and  salts  (ex.  Arsenic,  mercury,  lead,   cadmium,  copper,  iron  and  zinc;  toxic    metals  such  as  mercury  and  lead)   •  Radioactive  materials  –  examples  include  radioactive  radium  and  uranium  which  can   found  in  small  amounts  in  almost  all  rock    and  soil,  and  can  dissolve  in  water.   
Primary  Causes  of  Water   
Pollution   
•  Industrial  Wastes   •  Oil  Spill   •  Sewage  and  Waste  Water   •  Lake,  Ocean,  and  Marine  Dumping   •  Underground  Storage  and  Leakages  •  Pesticides  and  Fertilizers   •  Leaking  Landfills   •  Animal  Waste  
Effects  of  Water  Pollution   
•  Eutrophication  -  accumulation  of    nutrients  in  lakes  that  results  to  algal    blooms   •  Increased  organic  wastes  increase    biochemical  oxygen  demand.  This    reduces  the  oxygen  available  for    oxygen  dependent  aquatic  lifeforms.  
Effects  of  Water  Pollution   
•  Sedimentation  or  deposition  of    
sediment  in  surface  waters.    •  Sediments  destroy  spawning  and    breeding  grounds  for  fish,  smoother    fish  eggs  and  fry  and  thus  reduces    fish  and  shellfish  populations.    •  Suspended  sediments  decrease  light    penetration  in  water  bodies,  which    may  impair  the  growth  of  aquatic    plants  
Effects  of  Water  Pollution   
•  Thermal  pollution  -  lowers  the  dissolved   oxygen  content  of  water  at  the  same  time   
increasing  the  metabolic  rate  of  aquatic    organisms   •  It  can  cause  thermal  shock,  which  is  the   sudden  death  of  fish  and  other  organisms   that  cannot  escape  in  cooler  neighboring    waters   •  It  can  also  increase  the  susceptibility  of    aquatic  organisms  to  parasites,  certain  toxic   substances  and  pathogens   
Laws  Related  to  Water   Resources  Philippine  National  Standards  For   
Drinking  Water   
•  The  Philippine  National  Standards  for  Drinking  Water  (PNSDW)   is  a  set  of  guidelines  and  requirements  established  by  the   Department  of  Health  (DOH)  to  ensure  the  quality  and  safety  of   drinking  water  in  the  Philippines  (1963).    •  The  PNSDW  covers  all  aspects  of  drinking  water  management,   from  the  source  of  water  to  its  distribution  and  consumption.    •  It  sets  standards  for  physical,  chemical,  and  microbiological   parameters  that  must  be  met  by  drinking  water  providers  to   ensure  that  the  water  is  safe  for  human  consumption.  
DENR  A.O.  34  series  of  1990   
•  Entitled  the  “Water  Usage  and  Classification/Water  Quality   Criteria  and  Standards."   •  This  policy  establishes  guidelines  for  the  classification  and   management  of  Philippine  waters  based  on  their  designated   use.    •  It  sets  water  quality  criteria  and  standards  for  various  beneficial   uses,  including  domestic,  agricultural,  and  industrial  purposes,   and  provides  measures  for  the  prevention,  control,  and   abatement  of  water  pollution.

What  is  EIA?   
•It  is  a  systematic  study  of  the  relationship  between  a  project  and  the   
environment.
 
 •It  is  a  process  whereby  an  estimate  is  made  of  the  environmental   
consequences  which  may  be  expected  to  result  from  a  proposed   development  activity  or  project.   •It  determines  what  influence  a  project  will  have  on  the  environment   and  how  the  environment  will  affect  the  project.  
Environmental  Impact  Assessment   
•  EIA  is  a  planning  and  management    tool  that  will  help  government,  the    proponent,  the  affected    communities  and  other  decision    makers  assess  whether  the    

benefits  of  the  project  will  outweigh    the  negative  consequences  or  risks    on  the  environment  
Environmental  Impact  Assessment   
•  As  a  planning  tool,  it  helps  planners:   •  where  to  locate  projects   •  how  such  projects  should  be  designed   •  determine  the  operational  constraints  that  will  be  encountered  •  minimize  adverse  environmental  impacts.   •  As  a  management  tool,  it  is  used  for  collecting  and  analyzing   information  about  the  environmental  effects  of  projects  so  as  to  help   in  implementing  decisions.  
Objectives   
•  Understand  the  likely  consequences  of  new  projects  or  activities  on   the  environment   •Identify  measures  which  can  minimize  these  consequences  •  Provide  sufficient  data  for  decision  making

Impacts   
•  The  impact  of  an  activity  is  the  change  from  the  baseline  situation   caused  by  the  activity .   •  To  measure  an  impact,  you  must  know  what  the  baseline  situation  

is.  •Impacts  can  be  planned  or  unplanned  
Baseline  Situation   
•  The  baseline  situation  is  the  existing  environmental  situation  or   condition  in  the  absence  of  the  activity.   •  The  environmental  components  of  interest  are  those:  •likely  to  be  affected  by  your  activity   •  upon  which  your  activity  depends  for  its  success   •  For  example:  water,  soil,  fauna,  flora,  environmental  health,  special   ecosystems    •  The  baseline  situation  is  not  just  a  “snapshot  in  time”  but  is  a  process   that  has  variability  and  trend  over  time

Development   
•  Development,  in  a  report  from  UN,  is   defined  as  an  increase  in  well-being  across   the  members  of  a  society  between  two   points  in  time.    •  This  in  turn,  refers  to  any  activity  that  can   improve  the  lives  or  give  benefits  to  people   

over  a  period  of  time   •  However,  development  may  not  always   ‘sustainable’  
Sustainable  Development   
•  The  concept  of  “sustainable  development”    was  popularized  as  a  normative  goal  by    the  World  Commission  on  Environment    and  Development  the  in  their  1987  report    to  the  General  Assembly  of  the  United    Nations  Our  Common  Future.   •  According  to  UN  World  Commission  on    Environment  and  Development  (1987),    sustainable  development  was  defined  as  a    development  that  “ meets  the  needs  of  the    present  without  compromising  the  ability  of    

future  generations  to  meet  their  own    needs ”  
Sustainable  Development   
•  Sustainable  development  endeavors  to  strike   a  harmonious  equilibrium  among  economic   development,  environmental  protection,   and  social  well-being.   •  Sustainable  development  seeks  to  achieve   human  development  objectives  while  ensuring   that  natural  systems  can  continue  to  supply   essential  resources  and  ecosystem  services  to   support  human  well-being.   •  Its  ultimate  goal  is  to  create  a  society  where   

the  fulfillment  of  human  needs  and  the    preservation  of  the  planet's  integrity  and    stability  go  hand  in  hand.

Renewable  Energy   
•  Renewable  energy  refers  to  energy  sources  that  are  naturally  replenished  and  have  a   minimal  impact  on  the  environment.    •  These  energy  sources  are  derived  from  natural  processes  that  occur  continuously  or   can  be  naturally  replenished  within  a  relatively  short  period  typically  within  a  human   timescale.   •  Renewable  energy  plays  a  critical  role  in  promoting  sustainable  development  by   addressing  multiple  environmental,  social,  and  economic  challenges  
 
Renewable  Energy   
•  Solar  Energy:  Solar  energy  harnesses  the  power  of  sunlight  and  converts  it  into   usable  electricity  or  heat.    •  Wind  Energy:  Wind  turbines  convert  the  kinetic  energy  of  the  wind  into  electricity.  As   the  wind  blows,  it  rotates  the  turbine  blades,  which  spin  a  generator  to  produce   electricity.    •  Hydropower:  Hydropower  harnesses  the  energy  of  moving  or  falling  water  to  generate   electricity.  It  involves  the  use  of  dams  or  flowing  water  to  turn  turbines  and  produce   electricity.    •  Biomass:  Biomass  energy  is  derived  from  organic  matter  such  as  wood,  crop   residues,  agricultural  waste,  or  dedicated  energy  crops.  It  can  be  used  for  heat   generation  or  converted  into  biogas  or  biofuels  to  produce  electricity  or  power   vehicles.   •  Geothermal  Energy:  Geothermal  energy  utilizes  heat  from  within  the  Earth.   Geothermal  power  plants  extract  steam  or  hot  water  from  underground  and  convert  it   into  electricity.  
Sustainable  Agriculture   
•  Sustainable  Agriculture  refers  to  the  practice  of  doing  agricultural  activities  with   sustainability  in  mind.  This  can  be  done  by  management  of  resources  or  based  on  an   understanding  of  ecosystem  services.    •  Examples  of  agricultural  practices  that  promote  sustainability:    •  Reduction  of  chemical  use  in  farming   •  Flexible  business  process  and  farming  practices   •  Organic  farming   •  Resource  conservation   •  Crop  rotation,  multiple  cropping,  agroforestry  and  permaculture   •  Agrobiodiversity  conservation   •  Sustainable  livestock  management  
Energy  Efficiency   
•  Energy  efficiency  involves  utilizing   reduced  energy  consumption  to   accomplish  a  specific  task  or  achieve  a   desired  outcome.   •  Homes  and  buildings  that  prioritize  energy   efficiency  consume  less  energy  for  heating,   cooling,  and  operating  various  appliances   and  electronic  devices.    •  Industries  that  prioritize  energy  efficiency   consume  less  energy  during  the  production   process  of  goods.   •  This  reduces  energy  consumption,   lowers  greenhouse  gas  emissions,   lowers  cost  requirements,  conserves   energy  resources,  and  improves  indoor   air  quality.  
Circular  Economy  and  Cleaner  Production   
•  Cleaner  production  centers    on  enhancing  the    environmental  performance    of  production  processes  and    minimizing  the  ecological    impact  of  industrial    activities.    •  This  helps  reduce  or    suggest  better  choices  in    use  of  materials  and  energy,    and  to  avoid  waste,  waste    water  generation,  and    
gaseous  emissions,  and    also  waste  heat  and  noise.  
Circular  Economy  and  Cleaner  Production   
•  Circular  economy    minimizes  wastes  and    maximizes  resource    efficiency.    •  The  idea  is  to  reduce    the  finite  resources  that    are  being  utilized,  thus    reducing  waste.  Raw    materials  and  wastes    
are  also  kept  for  as  long    as  possible  through    reusing,  reducing  and    recycling.   
Green  Design   
•  Also  known  as  environmentally  sustainable   design  or  eco-design  is  a  design  philosophy  that   incorporates  environmental  considerations  into   the  design  and  development  of  products,   buildings,  systems,  and  processes.    •  Some  factors  considered  for  green  design   includes:   •  Environmental  footprint   •  Material  selection   •  Energy  efficiency   
•  Life  cycle  assessment   •  Design  and  disassembly   •  Biophilic  design   •  Water  efficiency   •  Social  considerations   
Education  and  Awareness   
•  Education  helps  individuals  develop  an    understanding  of  environmental    challenges,  such  as  climate  change,    biodiversity  loss,  resource  depletion,    and  pollution.   •  It  raises  awareness  about  the    interconnectedness  of  human  activities    
and  the  environment,  highlighting  the    need  for  sustainable  practices.   •  It  also  prepares  individuals  for  green    careers  and  fosters  innovation  in    sustainability-related  fields.   
Policy  and  Governance   
•  Lawmakers  and  implementers  have  a   crucial  role  in  sustainable    development  from  a  local  to  national    and  to  a  global  scale.  They  set  the    goals  and  provide  the  plan  that  will    help  a  nation  achieve  
sustainability.    •  This  can  range  from  addressing    environmental  problems  (such  as    pollution,  climate  change  and    conservation  of  natural  resources),    promoting  social  equity,  economic    instruments,  and  to  imposing  of    regulations  and  its  monitoring.

Measures  of  Sustainable  Development   
•  Well-being  has  much  potential  for  measuring  sustainable  development  if  it  is   broadened  beyond  its  traditional  scope  in  economics.   •  In  economics,  well-being  is  defined  as  the  discounted  present  value  of  future  utility.   However,  not  all  benefits  can  be  measured  in  monetary  terms.   
•  It  may  include  a  mix  of  different  factors  that  quantifies  how  a  project,  organization  or   nation  achieve  sustainability  in  terms  of  social,  economic,  and  environmental  aspect.   •  Some  of  the  measures  include   •  Human  Development  Index  (HDI) :  The  HDI  is  a  composite  measure  that  takes  into   account  factors  such  as  life  expectancy,  education,  and  income.    •  Gender  Inequality  Index  (GII):  The  GII  measures  gender-based  inequalities  in   reproductive  health,  empowerment,  and  economic  status.  It  considers  indicators  such   as  maternal  mortality  rates,  women's  representation  in  politics  and  the  workforce,  and   educational  attainment.  
Measures  of  Sustainable  Development   
•  Multidimensional  Poverty  Index  (MPI):  The  MPI  measures  poverty  by  considering   multiple  dimensions  beyond  income,  such  as  health,  education,  and  standard  of  living.   It  identifies  individuals  or  households  who  experience  simultaneous  deprivations  across   these  dimensions.    •  Environmental  Performance  Index  (EPI):  The  EPI  ranks  countries'  performance  in   
environmental  sustainability.  It  assesses  indicators  related  to  environmental  health,  air   quality,  water  and  sanitation,  biodiversity,  and  climate  change  mitigation.    •  Ecological  Footprint:  The  concept  of  ecological  footprint  measures  the  impact  of   human  activities  on  the  environment  by  assessing  the  amount  of  resources  and  land   required  to  sustain  a  population  or  an  individual's  consumption  patterns.  It  provides  a   means  to  evaluate  the  sustainability  of  resource  use  and  to  compare  the  ecological   demands  of  different  regions  or  countries.
